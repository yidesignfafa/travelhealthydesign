I led the 0-to-1 design of this public health platform to solve a systemic "Data Acquisition Problem." Executing 90% of the hands-on work, I bridged the gap between clinical rigor and user behavior, transforming a fragmented paper workflow into a live digital defense system.

THE PROBLEM

A Systemic Disconnect

The Context: During the COVID-19 crisis, the CDC needed to track virus variants across borders. However, the existing infrastructure was broken. It was a siloed system where researchers and travelers operated in completely different worlds.

The Core Problem: Researchers relied on manual paper forms that caused massive delays, while travelers faced high-friction processes that led to immediate drop-offs.

Part 1: The Input Engine

Solving Friction with
"Minimum Viable Data"

Designed the mobile app to remove barriers for the patients.

The Problem: 83% Drop-off Rate. That was the reality of the old system. The clinical requirements forced travelers to fill out 4-page medical history forms before they even arrived.

The Pivot: I facilitated a workshop to negotiate a "Minimum Viable Data" strategy, cutting initial fields by 30% and deferring complex questions.

The Core Loop:
The 60-Second Survey

We redesigned the intake flow into a bite-sized, linear loop that takes less than 60 seconds.

By reducing cognitive load and focusing on speed, we dramatically increased completion rates for travelers on the go.

🔄

Solving Retention:
Value Exchange

To solve the "Survey Fatigue" issue, we had to shift from "Extracting Data" to "Providing Value."

Emotional (Gamification): I designed the 'Digital Postcard' system. It turned data entry into a collectible game.

🧩

Clinical Utility (The Calendar): Created a Visual Health Diary that travelers use to share history with their doctors.

Solving Complexity:
Automating Logic

Malaria medication logic is notoriously confusing. Instead of exposing the complexity, I designed a Conditional Logic Controller.

The system uses the user's GPS and Itinerary to pre-select the correct drug schedule in the backend, reducing 7 manual decisions to 1 simple confirmation.

Part 2: The Output Engine

Visualizing Risk for
Rapid Response

For researchers, value meant insight, not just collection.

The Conflict: Stakeholders were drowning in noise. Policymakers needed macro trends to allocate funding, while epidemiologists needed granular, row-level data for contact tracing. The dashboard had to serve both.

The Solution: I designed a Risk-Density Visualization Model. We used a "Bubble" syntax where Size = Confidence and Color = Severity. This allowed an analyst to scan the entire globe in seconds.
